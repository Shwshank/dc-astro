Moving to Delhi changes the shape of an ordinary day.

A grocery delivery may involve a call from the gate. A five-kilometre journey may be easier by Metro at one hour and by cab at another. The small shop near your home may prefer a QR payment to the international card in your wallet. None of these moments is especially difficult, but together they can make the first few weeks feel more demanding than expected.

The good news is that Delhi becomes easier quite quickly. The turning point is not learning everything about the city. It is understanding the few everyday systems that make life run smoothly: how people describe an address, choose transport, pay, receive deliveries and prepare for the occasional problem.

For many expats, this requires a subtle change of habit. In much of the US, an exact street address and a card can carry you through most of the day. In many European cities, a transport pass, a walkable centre and contactless payment may do the same. Delhi is different. Here, everyday confidence comes from combining digital convenience with local context—and keeping more than one option available.

## Your address is more than an address

One of the first things newcomers notice is that a technically correct Delhi address is not always a practically useful one.

In an American suburb, a house number and ZIP code will usually take a driver to the right door. Across much of Europe, a street name and building number often work equally well. Delhi has formal addresses too, but large residential colonies, several gates, towers with similar names and entrances on unexpected roads can make the final few hundred metres the most complicated part of a journey.

This is why Delhi residents often add a landmark: opposite a market, near a particular gate, behind a well-known hotel. It is not a replacement for the address; it is the last piece of navigation.

When you move into a new home, save a version of your address that another person can actually use. Include the precise map pin, gate or tower, floor, nearby landmark and any instruction from building security. Send that version to drivers, visitors and delivery staff.

This small adjustment prevents a surprising amount of daily friction. It also makes a new home begin to feel established: other people can find you without a long series of phone calls.

## Delhi asks you to choose your journey each time

Newcomers often want to identify the single “best” way to travel around Delhi. There is no permanent answer—and that is worth accepting early.

In cities such as London, Paris or Berlin, public transport is often the default for a wide range of journeys. In many US cities, the car occupies that role. Delhi rewards a more flexible approach. The Metro may be the most predictable option for a cross-city journey, while a cab or auto may be better for the first or final stretch. Rain, heat, luggage, road traffic and walking distance can all change the sensible choice.

The useful habit is to compare, not assume. Before leaving, look at both the road journey and the Metro route. Ask which one offers the better balance of time, comfort and effort on that particular day.

Official app-based QR ticketing is available for Delhi Metro journeys, including through the Delhi Metro Sarthi app. That can feel familiar to travellers used to mobile transit tickets in Europe or the US. The difference is that Delhi’s best journey may still combine several modes: a short cab to the station, the Metro across the city and an auto for the last section.

It is wise to set up at least two transport options and try each one on a day when you are not rushing. A practice journey teaches you more than a list of instructions: where your building’s pickup point is, how long the walk from the station really takes and whether your mobile connection works along the route.

## The payment culture is digital—but not necessarily card-first

Payment is one of the clearest examples of how modern Delhi may differ from what a newcomer expects.

An American may be used to tapping or inserting a credit card for almost everything. A European expat may expect contactless cards to work from a café to a train station. Cards are widely useful in Delhi too, particularly in hotels, larger shops and restaurants. But much of everyday payment culture is built around UPI: scanning a QR code and paying directly from a compatible account or wallet.

You may see the same QR-payment habit at a neighbourhood fruit seller, a pharmacy and a polished boutique. This can initially feel unusual to someone whose phone wallet is linked mainly to overseas cards. Once available, however, it makes small payments remarkably simple.

New residents with an eligible Indian bank account and mobile number can ask their bank about UPI activation. International visitors and NRIs may be eligible for UPI One World, which NPCI describes as a prepaid UPI wallet that can be loaded using an international debit or credit card. Eligibility and onboarding arrangements should be checked when needed, as services change.

The practical lesson is not to choose one payment method and abandon the others. Delhi works best with a small combination: a digital method for routine payments, a working card and modest cash for backup. Test each with a low-value purchase before depending on it.

## Delivery convenience depends on a little human context

Delhi’s delivery economy can make a new home functional very quickly. Groceries, meals, household items and many routine pharmacy needs can arrive at the door. For an expat facing unfamiliar shops, a busy work schedule or a half-unpacked kitchen, that convenience is genuinely liberating.

The experience may feel more conversational than delivery in parts of Europe or the US. A rider may call to confirm the gate, ask for a landmark or wait while building security contacts you. This is not necessarily a failure of the system. It is often how the digital order connects with the physical complexity of the city.

Your first order should therefore be small. Use it to learn how your building works. Does security need your approval? Is the entrance shown by the map pin actually open? Does “leave at the door” suit the building’s rules, or is reception the normal handover point?

Once these details are saved correctly, delivery becomes almost effortless. Until then, keeping your phone nearby is part of the transaction.

There is also no need to recreate your entire former household in one order. Begin with what makes the first days comfortable—drinking water, simple food, toiletries, cleaning basics and any necessary adapters. Delhi has its own brands, package sizes and shopping rhythms. Discovering them gradually is part of settling in, not a problem to solve overnight.

## A local mobile number quietly unlocks the city

The smartphone may look like the centre of everyday life in Delhi, but the Indian mobile number behind it is what makes many services work.

One-time passwords sent by SMS are commonly used to create or verify accounts. Drivers and couriers may need to call. Building security, a local service provider or a new contact may assume you can receive an ordinary Indian call, not only a message through an internet-based app.

For someone arriving from the US or Europe with international roaming, this can be easy to underestimate. Roaming gives you connectivity, but it does not always give you the local identity that services expect.

Arrange a suitable local connection early and confirm the current documentation requirements with the provider. Then test the unglamorous basics: an incoming call, an SMS code and mobile data away from Wi-Fi. These checks matter more than downloading twenty apps that you cannot fully activate.

## Prepare for an ordinary problem before it becomes urgent

Feeling at home in a city is partly about knowing what to do when the day does not go to plan.

Save one nearby hospital, a licensed pharmacy, your insurer or employer’s medical-assistance contact and the details of any regular prescription. Keep a short health note on your phone with allergies, current medicines and an emergency contact. This is not dramatic preparation; it is the same kind of quiet readiness as knowing your nearest urgent-care centre in the US or local pharmacy in Europe.

Create a second note that is available offline. Include your full home address, building security, a trusted local contact, your employer or relocation adviser and your embassy or high commission. India’s unified emergency number is **112**; New Delhi’s district administration also lists **101** for fire and rescue, **102** for ambulance, **1077** for disaster support and **1091** as the women’s helpline.

Numbers and service arrangements can change, so official sources should always be checked. The point is to have the information before stress makes it harder to search.

## The real goal is not a perfectly organised phone

Everyday Delhi is not made easy by having the longest app list. It becomes easy when the city stops demanding a fresh decision every few minutes.

Your driver can find the correct gate. You know whether to take the Metro or the road. You can pay in more than one way. The delivery rider can reach you. Important contacts are available even if the data connection is not.

These are small systems, but they create something larger: independence. They give newcomers the mental space to notice their neighbourhood, establish a favourite café, learn a regular route and begin building a life beyond arrival logistics.

Delhi may not operate exactly like the city you left in Europe or the US. It is often more layered, more conversational and more dependent on context. Once you understand that rhythm, it can also be extraordinarily convenient.
